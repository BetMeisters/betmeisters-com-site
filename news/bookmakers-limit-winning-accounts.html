News · Account restrictions

Why bookmakers limit winning accounts, what the regulator's data shows, and what you can do

Hundreds of thousands of UK betting accounts are cut back or closed every year, and winners are far more likely to be hit. Here is how restrictions work, what the rules say and your real options.

Horse racingFootballSports bettingLicensing and regulationGeneral news
By the BetMeisters desk16 November 202610 min read

If you have ever placed a bet and found the bookmaker would only let you stake a few pounds, you have met an account restriction. Punters call it being 'limited', 'stake-factored' or 'gubbed'. It is one of the most common complaints in British betting, and for years the only evidence was anecdote. That changed in July 2025, when the UK Gambling Commission published data on restrictions across almost 15 million betting accounts.

This guide explains what the data shows, why bookmakers restrict accounts, what the law does and does not say, and what you can sensibly do about it. It does not tell you how to dodge restrictions with extra accounts or someone else's name. That breaks bookmakers' terms, can get winnings withheld, and is something we will never recommend.

What is an account restriction?

A restriction is any limit a bookmaker puts on a customer for its own commercial reasons. It is not the same as the checks a bookmaker must make for money-laundering or financial risk, or the limits you set yourself as a safer-gambling tool. The Commission's data separates out four main types:

  • Stake factoring. The bookmaker cuts the maximum stake it will accept from you to a percentage of what an ordinary customer can bet. A stake factor of 10% means that where a normal customer could bet £500, you can bet £50.
  • Account closure. The bookmaker closes the account for commercial reasons. You get your balance back, but you cannot bet.
  • Betting withdrawn, account kept open. A stake factor of zero. You can still log in and perhaps use the casino, but you cannot place sports bets.
  • Market restrictions. You are blocked from particular markets, most often horse racing, but can still bet elsewhere.

A single account can be hit by more than one of these, and many punters hold several accounts, so the number of restricted accounts is not the same as the number of people affected.

What the Gambling Commission's data shows

In early 2025 the Commission asked some of the largest online betting operators for data on every active account in 2024. That meant every account that placed at least one bet that year: 14,923,840 accounts in total. The results were published in a blog by its chief executive, Andrew Rhodes, on 23 July 2025.

The headline: 643,779 accounts, or 4.31%, were restricted in some form. Of those restricted accounts:

  • 62.17% had a stake-factor restriction, the most common type;
  • 51.69% were closed for commercial reasons;
  • 19.15% had betting withdrawn while the account stayed open;
  • 5.72% were restricted from particular markets only.

The stake factors are severe. Among restricted accounts, 22.41% were cut to 1% or less of the normal maximum stake, 36.22% to between 1% and 9%, and 29.43% to between 10% and 49%. Only around one in seven could still bet half or more of the normal stake. In other words, once a bookmaker decides to limit you, it usually limits you hard.

The most telling figure is who gets restricted. Across all active accounts, 25.42% were in profit over their lifetime. Among restricted accounts, 46.78% were in profit. Restricted customers were almost twice as likely to be winning. That confirms what punters have long said: restrictions are aimed mainly at people who win, or who bet in a way that looks likely to win.

Is 4% the real figure?

Many regular punters say the official figure feels low. The Racing Post's Big Punting Survey, answered by almost 10,000 bettors and published in February 2025, found that 31.9% had been restricted on one or more accounts in the previous 12 months, up from 25.6% in the survey's first edition in 2023. Including older restrictions, the figure rose to 43.6%.

The two numbers measure different things, and both can be true. The Commission counts accounts, including millions of casual accounts that place a few small bets a year and would never be restricted. The survey counts committed punters who chose to answer a betting survey: exactly the group most likely to bet in a way bookmakers watch. For the casual customer, restrictions are rare. For the regular, price-aware punter, they are common.

Why bookmakers restrict accounts

A traditional bookmaker makes money from the margin built into its prices. Across a whole market, the prices add up to more than 100%, and that extra is the bookmaker's edge. Our own price data shows how big it is: on 16 November 2026 the average margin on main markets ranged from about 8.5% at bet365 to about 10.2% at Paddy Power, across the four big UK firms we track.

That model works when most customers bet at a disadvantage. It breaks when a customer consistently beats the price, for example:

  • Taking early prices that later shorten, a sign the punter knows something the price did not reflect;
  • Betting only when one bookmaker is out of line with the rest of the market, often called value betting or arbing;
  • Using offers systematically, such as matched betting, so the bookmaker pays out offer money without making margin back;
  • Following sharp information in small markets where the bookmaker's prices are weaker.

The Commission's own explanation is blunt: operators act in their commercial interests and manage their liabilities, and restriction rates vary with each operator's risk appetite, size and customer base. A large bookmaker that takes the market on prices it sets itself will act differently from one that copies others' prices.

Is it legal?

Yes, in Great Britain, as long as it is done fairly and openly. The Commission has said it is not in its remit to dictate how operators manage their commercial liabilities, and that its data request did not change that position. A business can choose its customers, provided it does not discriminate on protected characteristics such as race or sex. Being a successful bettor is not a protected characteristic.

There is also no 'minimum bet' rule in Britain. Some Australian states require bookmakers to accept bets on racing up to a set amount to win. The 2023 white paper on reforming the Gambling Act did not propose anything similar, and nothing has been introduced since.

The Commission has, however, set out what it expects. Operators should be transparent at the start and throughout the relationship about how, when and why an account might be restricted. Customers should know about restrictions before they deposit or bet, and an operator whose business model involves severe restrictions should say so. The Commission has also said it is concerned that restrictions can push customers towards illegal, unlicensed sites, or into opening accounts in other people's names. Both undermine the protections that come with a licence.

When a restriction turns into a dispute

A restriction on future bets is a commercial decision. A refusal to pay a bet that has already been accepted is a different matter, and that is where you have more protection.

One case reported in the UK press shows the line. A winning racing customer said bet365 limited her to stakes of £1 and froze a balance of about £54,000 while a dispute over her account was resolved. The case went to IBAS, the independent dispute service bet365 uses, and bet365 said it would abide by the ruling. The point is not who was right. It is that frozen winnings go through a formal complaints process with an independent body at the end, while a stake factor on future bets usually does not.

Under UK licence conditions, every licensed operator must have a complaints process and must name an approved alternative dispute resolution (ADR) provider, such as IBAS or eCOGRA. If your complaint about a settled bet, a voided bet or withheld winnings is not resolved by the operator, normally within eight weeks, you can take it to that ADR provider free of charge.

What you can actually do

There is no trick that makes restrictions go away, but there are honest ways to protect yourself.

  • Read the terms before you deposit. Look for the sections on maximum stakes, maximum winnings, 'irregular betting' and the right to void bets. If a bookmaker gives itself wide powers to cancel winning bets, that matters more than its welcome offer. It is why every BetMeisters.news review has a Terms section, written good or bad.
  • Use betting exchanges. On an exchange such as Betfair Exchange, Smarkets or Matchbook you bet against other customers, and the exchange earns commission on winnings instead of margin on prices. Exchanges do not restrict winners for winning, although very high earners can face extra charges, and prices in small markets can be thin.
  • Keep records. Screenshots of bet slips, stakes offered and any messages about your account make a complaint far easier if winnings are ever withheld.
  • Complain properly when money is at stake. Use the operator's complaints process first, in writing, then the ADR provider named in its terms.
  • Do not open accounts in other people's names or use VPNs to get round limits. It breaks the terms, it is the most common reason for confiscated winnings, and it can put you outside the protection of the licence.
  • Watch your own limits. A restriction is not a challenge to win your money back elsewhere. If being limited makes you chase, set deposit limits or take a break: support is available 24/7 from the National Gambling Helpline on 0808 8020 133.

FAQs

Why has my betting account been limited? Usually because the bookmaker thinks you are likely to win over time, for example by taking early prices, betting when its price is out of line, or using offers systematically. Bookmakers rarely give a specific reason.

Can a bookmaker legally limit my stakes? Yes. In Great Britain the Gambling Commission does not control how operators manage their commercial risk, as long as they do not discriminate on protected characteristics and are open about their policies.

Will I get my balance back if my account is closed? A licensed operator should return your balance. If it withholds money, complain in writing and then take the dispute to the ADR provider named in its terms.

Do betting exchanges restrict winners? Exchanges make money from commission, not from customers losing, so they do not restrict customers for winning. Some charge extra fees to their most profitable customers.

How many accounts get restricted? In the Commission's 2024 data, 643,779 of 14.9 million active accounts, or 4.31%. Among regular punters answering a Racing Post survey, nearly a third said they had been restricted in the previous year.
