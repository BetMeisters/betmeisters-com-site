Telegram betting groups: useful, pointless or a con? How to tell in five minutes

Most Telegram tipster channels show you their winners and hide the rest. Police, regulators and industry research all point to the same warning signs: guaranteed wins, fixed matches, paid VIP rooms and links to unlicensed bookmakers. Here is what a channel worth following looks like.

Safer gamblingEnforcementSports betting
By the BetMeisters desk24 November 20265 min read

Telegram is full of betting channels. Some post a tip or two a day and move on. Others promise "sure" wins, sell VIP access or claim to know the result of a fixed match before it is played. From the outside they can look alike: a logo, a run of green ticks and screenshots of winning slips.

The test that separates them is simple, and it is not whether the last five tips won. It is whether you can see every tip, posted before the start, with the price and the stake, and what happened to it.

What the police say

The UK's national fraud reporting service, Report Fraud, lists betting fraud as a scam type in its own right: people selling insider tips or "foolproof" betting systems, and offers to bet on your behalf. Its advice is blunt: "Beware of any scheme that guarantees you will win at betting." It also asks the obvious question: if someone really had a winning secret, why would they sell it?

Fixed-match groups

The worst channels claim that games are fixed and that, for a fee, you can bet on the result. In 2023 Dutch media reported on Telegram "match-fixing" groups, some with hundreds of thousands of members, promoted by influencers with pictures of supercars and Dubai flats. Young people paid in and lost thousands of euros, and NL Times reported that most victims did not go to the police.

A real fixer would not sell the result to thousands of strangers on an open channel. If a group says it knows the score in advance, you can assume it is a con. If it were true, you would be paying to take part in a crime.

Regulators are watching, but often cannot act

In March 2025 the Dutch gambling regulator, the Kansspelautoriteit (KSA), raised concerns about a popular Telegram tipster channel, worried about its effect on young people who saw tipsters as role models. Two licensed operators stopped their Telegram campaigns. But the KSA said it had no power over tipsters who were not licensed, and was waiting for new law.

In the UK, the Gambling Commission prosecuted a man who ran an illegal bookmaking business through WhatsApp. In December 2025 he was given a suspended prison sentence and ordered to pay £230,000 in compensation, after failing to pay a customer a balance of £269,000. The Commission's director of enforcement said the case showed "all the risks that consumers face from illegal gambling". Messaging apps are not the problem in themselves. The problem is that they make it easy to take bets, and money, without any licence.

Follow the money: affiliate links

Many free channels earn money from the bookmaker link in their bio rather than from their tips. An Irish Times investigation in 2019 found that tipster affiliates could earn 20% to 40% of what referred customers lost. The money comes in whether the tips win or lose, which removes much of the reason to be honest about results. Betting analyst Joseph Buchdahl's advice, quoted there, still stands: if a tipster says they can beat the market, they should prove it.

The links do not always go to licensed bookmakers either. Research published by Entain in June 2026 found 44 influencer and tipster accounts across seven platforms promoting more than 30 unregulated gambling sites to UK users. At least 12 fan and tipster accounts posted the same tips at the same time, a sign of a co-ordinated affiliate campaign, mostly without saying it was an advert. The report said the promotions were aimed at young men aged 14 to 25.

Red flags

  • Guaranteed wins, "100% sure", "no-lose" or "fixed" anything.
  • A paid VIP room where the "real" tips are, with the free channel used as an advert for it.
  • Only winning slips. Screenshots are easy to fake, and losing tips quietly deleted are the oldest trick there is.
  • Profit claims with no dates or stakes: "+87 units" means nothing without the period, the number of tips and the stake on each.
  • A bookmaker link you are pushed to use, especially one for a site that is not licensed where you live.
  • Offers to bet for you, "account management" or asking you to send money to an individual.
  • Luxury lifestyle posts (cars, watches, cash) instead of a record.

What a channel worth following looks like

  • Every tip is posted before the event starts, with the price and the stake.
  • There is a permanent record somewhere you can check, with losing tips left in.
  • Results are posted win or lose, and the running profit or loss is shown at the same stake.
  • It says clearly whether it earns money from links, and does not push you to sign up anywhere.
  • It talks about chances and prices, not certainties, and points you to safer-gambling help.

Even a good channel will have long losing runs. A tipster with a real edge of a few per cent will still lose nearly half their bets at even money. That is why the record matters more than the last week.
