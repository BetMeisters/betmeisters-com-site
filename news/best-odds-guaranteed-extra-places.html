Best odds guaranteed and extra places explained: what they pay, what they are worth and the terms to check

Two of the most common racing concessions can make a real difference to what you are paid, but only if you understand the terms. Here is how they work, with each-way maths and real place terms from today's cards.

Horse racingSports bettingBonuses, rewards and VIP
By the BetMeisters desk18 November 20268 min read

Walk into any conversation about racing betting in Britain and two phrases come up: best odds guaranteed (BOG) and extra places. Both are concessions that bookmakers offer to win racing customers, and both can genuinely add to what you are paid. Neither is free money, and both come with terms. This guide explains how each one works, shows the each-way maths, and uses the place terms that bookmakers were offering on today's British cards.

As always, a better price is only worth having on a bet you meant to make anyway. 18+.

First, the starting price

In British and Irish racing, every runner has an official starting price (SP): its price at the moment the race starts. If you bet "at SP", you are paid at whatever that turns out to be. If you take a fixed price earlier (an early price, or the board price before the off) you are normally paid at that price, whatever happens to the SP.

Prices move all day as money comes in and as non-runners are announced. Some horses shorten, some drift. Taking an early price is a bet on the price as well as the horse.

What best odds guaranteed means

With BOG, if you take a fixed price and the SP turns out to be bigger, you are paid at the SP. If the SP is shorter, you keep the price you took. You get whichever is better.

  • You back a horse at 5/1 and it starts at 4/1: you are paid at 5/1.
  • You back it at 5/1 and it starts at 6/1: you are paid at 6/1.
  • You back it at 5/1 and it starts at 5/1: you are paid at 5/1.

In money: a £10 win bet at 5/1 that wins returns £60. If the horse drifted to 8/1 and you had BOG, it returns £90 instead. Without BOG it would still return £60.

BOG only matters when the horse wins (or places, for each-way bets), and only when it drifts. But it takes away one of the risks of betting early: that you take a price and the market then moves against you.

BOG and each-way bets

Where BOG applies to each-way bets, the better price is used for both parts, so the place part is paid at the place fraction of the better price. If you back a horse at 8/1 each way, it drifts to 10/1 and finishes second with 1/5 place terms, the place part is paid at 10/1 × 1/5 = 2/1 instead of 8/5.

The terms to check

BOG is a concession, not a rule, and every bookmaker sets its own terms. The common conditions are:

  • Which races: usually UK and Irish racing, sometimes only certain meetings or only from a certain time on the day of the race.
  • Which bets: usually win, place and each-way singles. Accumulators and some promotional bets are often excluded.
  • Odds limits: some bookmakers set a minimum price, and very short prices may not qualify.
  • Who gets it: some offers are new-customer only, and free bets may be excluded.
  • Limits: some bookmakers cap the extra amount BOG can add, or the stake it applies to.

If a bookmaker removes BOG from your account, the only sign may be that the offer stops appearing on your bet slip. Check before you bet, not after the race.

Non-runners, Rule 4 and BOG

When a horse is withdrawn after you have taken a price, the rest of the field has a better chance of winning, so the Tattersalls Committee's Rule 4(C) allows a deduction from your winnings. The size of the deduction depends on the withdrawn horse's price, from 90p in the pound for a 1/9 shot down to nothing for a horse over 14/1, and the total deduction is capped at 90p in the pound. Bets at SP are not affected, except when there was not enough time to form a new market.

This matters for BOG. After a non-runner, your fixed price may be reduced by Rule 4 while the SP is not, so the comparison is between your price after the deduction and the SP. How each bookmaker applies BOG when a Rule 4 deduction is in force is set out in its own terms. On Wednesday's cards, for example, Kalo Athena was taken out of the 1.33 at Hexham and My Gift To You out of the 2.38, both at prices around 6/1 to 10/1, so early bets on those races could carry small deductions.

Each-way place terms: the standard

To understand extra places, you need the standard terms first. Unless a bookmaker displays different terms, each-way bets in Britain follow Tattersalls Rule 3:

The terms are fixed by the number of runners when the bet is settled, which is why a non-runner can change the place terms. A handicap that drops from 16 runners to 15 goes from four places at 1/5 to three places at 1/4.

What extra places are

An extra place offer means the bookmaker pays one or more places beyond the standard. In a 14-runner handicap the standard is 1/4 the odds for three places; with an extra place, it might pay four places at 1/5.

Extra places are now offered on ordinary weekday races, not just big festivals. In the racecard data for Wednesday's British races, taken at 4pm UK on Tuesday:

  • Kempton 7.00 (14-runner handicap): of the 18 bookmakers showing each-way terms, 7 paid four places at 1/5 (10Bet, bet365, BetMGM, Betfred, BoyleSports, Unibet and William Hill) and 11 paid the standard three places at 1/4.
  • Ffos Las 2.18 (15-runner handicap chase): 10 of 19 paid four places at 1/5.
  • Kempton 8.00 and 8.30 (13-runner handicaps): 7 paid four places in each.
  • Hexham 3.08 (12-runner handicap chase): 5 paid four places.

Every other British race on Wednesday was offered on the standard terms across the board, apart from one firm paying three places at 1/5 in Kempton's seven-runner 7.30. So extra places are mostly a handicap concession, concentrated in the bigger fields, and they vary between bookmakers in the same race. They are announced race by race and can change, so check the terms on the bet slip.

Is an extra place worth it? The each-way maths

An extra place usually comes with a smaller fraction: four places at 1/5 instead of three at 1/4. So you gain a place but are paid less for each one. Here is a £5 each-way bet (£10 in total) at 10/1:

Finishes1/4, three places1/5, four places
1st£55 + £17.50 = £72.50£55 + £15 = £70
2nd or 3rd£17.50£15
4th£0£15
5th or worse£0£0

Which is better depends on how often the horse finishes fourth compared with how often it finishes in the first three. As an illustration only, take a horse with about a 9% chance of finishing in each of the first four positions. On the place part alone, the standard terms return about £0.95 per £1 staked (a 27% chance of £3.50), while the extra-place terms return about £1.08 (a 36% chance of £3). In this example the extra place turns a losing place bet into a winning one.

The extra place is worth most for horses with a real chance of finishing fourth but not much chance of winning: mid-priced runners in big handicaps. It is worth least for a short favourite, where the lower fraction cuts the place return and fourth place rarely comes into it.

One more point: the bookmakers paying extra places do not always have the best win price. A slightly bigger win price on standard terms can be worth more than an extra place on a shorter price, especially for horses near the front of the market.

BOG and extra places together

Many bookmakers offer both on the same race. Combined, they help with two separate risks: BOG protects you if the price drifts after you bet, and the extra place pays out on a fourth (or fifth) that would otherwise lose. But they are still bets with a bookmaker's margin built in, and the same terms apply: check the race qualifies, the bet type qualifies, and the place terms are the ones on your bet slip.

A quick checklist

  1. Is the race covered by BOG, and is your bet type included?
  2. What are the each-way terms: how many places, at what fraction?
  3. Is an extra place on offer, and is it on the bet slip?
  4. How does the win price compare across bookmakers? An extra place does not make up for a much shorter price.
  5. Has a horse been withdrawn? A Rule 4 deduction or a change in place terms can follow.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming BOG is always on. It is a concession with its own terms, and it can be removed from an account.
  • Forgetting the place fraction. An extra place at 1/5 pays less per place than three places at 1/4.
  • Chasing extra places on short favourites. The value is mainly in mid-priced runners in big fields.
  • Ignoring non-runners. A withdrawal can change the place terms and bring a Rule 4 deduction.

FAQs

Does BOG cost anything? No: you are simply paid at the better of your price and the SP. But it does not change the margin built into the prices.

Does BOG apply to accumulators? Often not. Check the bookmaker's terms.

Are extra places a promotion? They are a concession offered race by race. They usually apply to each-way bets on the named race only.

What happens to each-way terms if there is a non-runner? They are set by the number of runners when the bet is settled, so a withdrawal can reduce the number of places paid.
