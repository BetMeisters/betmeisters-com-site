Prediction markets explained: how Kalshi and Polymarket work, and where they are legal

A contract that pays $1 if something happens: how prediction-market prices turn into odds, how they differ from a bookmaker, the US court fight over sports contracts, and why they are not licensed for players in Great Britain.

Sports bettingRegulation
By the BetMeisters desk27 November 20266 min read

A prediction market lets you buy a contract that pays out if an event happens: a team winning, an election result, an interest-rate decision. In the US the best known are Kalshi and Polymarket, and sports have become their biggest products. To a bettor they look a lot like a betting exchange. To their operators they are financial contracts. That difference is the whole story, so this guide explains both sides.

How a contract works

Each contract usually pays $1 if the answer is Yes and nothing if it is No. You buy at a price between 1 cent and 99 cents, and in a busy market that price is generally read as the market's estimate of the chance the event happens. A contract trading at 62 cents says the market thinks it is about a 62% chance.

The conversion is simple: decimal odds = 1 ÷ price. So $10 spent on 40-cent contracts buys 25 contracts; if the event happens they pay $25, the same as a 2.50 bet. Before fees, that is. Platforms charge trading fees, and the price you can actually get depends on how many people are trading, so the real return is a little lower than the table shows.

How it differs from a bookmaker

  • You trade with other users. As on a betting exchange, someone else takes the other side. The platform makes its money from fees rather than from building a margin into the odds.
  • Prices move all the time and you can usually sell a contract before the event, much like cashing out, at whatever the market will pay.
  • Thin markets are a trap. On a smaller game there may be few people trading, so the gap between buying and selling prices can be wide.
  • Different protections. In the US these platforms sit under federal financial rules, not state gambling rules, which is exactly what the states are challenging (below).

The UK Gambling Commission makes the same point from the other side: the core features of a prediction market resemble a betting exchange, which has been licensed in Britain since 2000.

Who regulates them in the US

The federal regulator is the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Kalshi is a CFTC-registered exchange (a designated contract market). Polymarket was barred from US customers after a 2022 settlement with the CFTC; it bought a small CFTC-licensed exchange in July 2025 and returned to the US with a waitlisted app in December 2025, starting with sports.

In June 2026 the CFTC proposed rules that would treat most sports contracts as allowed, while saying some would likely be against the public interest: contracts on player injuries, officiating decisions, single plays, fights, and games below college level. A proposed rule is not yet final.

The fight with the states

Many states say a contract on who wins a football game is sports betting, whatever it is called, and that it needs a state licence. The federal appeal courts have split:

  • Third Circuit (New Jersey): sided with Kalshi in April 2026, finding federal law likely overrides the state's gambling law while the case continues.
  • Ninth Circuit (Nevada), August 2026 and Sixth Circuit (Ohio and Tennessee), 25 September 2026: held that states can apply their gambling laws to the contracts.
  • Supreme Court: New Jersey has asked the Court to review the Third Circuit decision. As of 25 November 2026 the Court has not said whether it will take the case.
  • New York: the attorney general is suing Kalshi; on 23 November 2026 a judge declined to order an immediate shutdown, so its markets stay open while the case runs, with a ruling expected early next year.
  • Tribal lands: on 25 November 2026 a federal judge ordered Kalshi to block sports contracts on the lands of four New Mexico tribes.

The practical result: whether these products are lawful where you are can depend on your state and on court rulings still to come. Our news piece Courts split on prediction markets covers the rulings in more detail.

What about the UK?

In February 2026 the Gambling Commission said prediction markets offered in Great Britain would likely count as a betting intermediary, a type of gambling business that needs a Commission licence, and that they could not classify themselves as non-gambling products. It warned that there are criminal offences for operating without a licence and that unlicensed operators should not target or take business from British customers.

So for a UK reader the honest position is: the US sports prediction markets are not licensed for you. If you want exchange-style prices, use a betting exchange that holds a Gambling Commission licence, which you can check on the Commission's public register.

FAQ

Is a prediction market the same as betting? In Great Britain the regulator says it likely is gambling and needs a licence. In the US that is the question the courts are deciding.

Can I use Kalshi or Polymarket from the UK? They are not licensed by the Gambling Commission for British customers. Do not try to get around location checks.

Are the prices better than a bookmaker's? Sometimes, because there is no built-in margin; but fees and thin markets can wipe that out. Always convert the price to odds and compare.

What does a 30c price mean? Roughly a 30% chance, or decimal odds of about 3.33 before fees.
