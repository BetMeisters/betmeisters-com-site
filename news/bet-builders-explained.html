Bet builders explained: how they are priced, why the margin is bigger, and when one makes sense

A bet builder lets you combine several outcomes from one match into a single bet. Here is how bookmakers price them, why correlation matters, why the margin is usually much bigger than on a single, and how to check one.

FootballSports bettingFootball
By the BetMeisters desk19 November 20267 min read

A bet builder (also called a 'same game multi' or 'request a bet') lets you combine several outcomes from one match into a single bet: for example, a team to win, over 2.5 goals and a named player to score. They are hugely popular in the UK because they turn one game into one big price. This guide explains how they are priced and why they usually cost you more than the same bets placed one at a time.

One point first: a bet builder is still a bet, and a long-odds one at that. Stake only what you can afford to lose. 18+.

How a normal accumulator is priced

In an ordinary accumulator the selections come from different matches, so they are independent: one result tells you nothing about another. The price is simply the individual prices multiplied together. A 2.00 shot and a 3.00 shot make a 6.00 double.

Why a bet builder is different: correlation

Outcomes in the same match are connected. If a team wins, it is more likely the game had goals; if a striker scores, his team is more likely to win. Multiplying the prices would therefore overstate the true odds, so bookmakers cannot do it. Instead they price the combination as a whole with their own model of how the outcomes move together.

A worked example. Say a team is 50% to win (fair price 2.00) and over 2.5 goals is also 50% (2.00). Multiplied, that is 4.00, implying 25%. But wins and goals go together, so the real chance of both might be nearer 32%, a fair price of about 3.13. A bookmaker offering 2.90 for the pair is not giving you a bigger price than the 4.00 the singles suggest; it is still charging a margin on top of the true 3.13.

Why the margin is bigger

On a main match-result market our own price scans show mainstream UK bookmakers building in a margin of roughly 4% to 8%. On a bet builder, the margin applies to every part of the combination and is hidden inside a correlation model you cannot see, so the total is usually far larger than on any single leg, and it grows with every leg you add, especially player props. You cannot see that margin directly, which is exactly why it is worth checking the legs one by one.

The simple rule: the more legs, and the more player markets, the more you pay for the convenience.

How to check a bet builder

  1. Price the legs as singles. Note each leg's best single price, ideally from a sharp bookmaker or the exchange.
  2. Ask whether they are connected. Legs that point the same way (a favourite to win and overs; a striker to score and his team to win) make the true price shorter than the singles multiplied. Legs that pull against each other (a team to win and under 1.5 goals) make it longer.
  3. Compare. If the builder price is close to, or above, what the singles multiplied would pay for connected legs, it may be fair. If it is well below, the margin is doing the work.
  4. Keep it short. Two connected legs at sensible prices can be reasonable; six legs with three player props almost never are.

When a bet builder makes sense

Bet builders are best treated as entertainment on a match you are watching, with a small stake. If you are trying to bet on value, singles on the main markets, where margins are lowest and prices are most efficient, are almost always the better route. The rules that protect you

In Great Britain, bet builders are offered by licensed bookmakers under the same Gambling Commission rules as any other bet: the terms must be clear, and you can set deposit limits, take a break or self-exclude at any time. If a bet builder is part of a promotion, the wider promotion rules apply too, including the cap on wagering requirements that came in on 19 January 2026.
