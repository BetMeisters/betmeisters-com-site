Does Britain need a new Gambling Act? An independent commission of economists wants your evidence

The Gambling Policy Commission, chaired by Cambridge economist Jagjit Chadha, has opened a review of the 2005 Act. It is not a government inquiry, but it is asking the questions the next round of reform will have to answer.

RegulationUK
By the BetMeisters desk18 November 20263 min read

More than twenty years after the Gambling Act 2005 became the legal backbone of betting and gambling in Great Britain, a new body has opened a review into whether it is still fit for purpose. The Gambling Policy Commission launched its review on 9 November and has asked researchers, consumers, the industry, regulators and policymakers to send evidence.

Who is behind it

The commission describes itself as independent of government, political parties, regulators and the industry. It is chaired by Professor Jagjit Chadha OBE, a Cambridge economist and former director of the National Institute of Economic and Social Research, with Professor James Reade of the University of Reading as chief economist. Its members come mostly from economics, with others from law, public policy, health and sport; several have advised the Bank of England or HM Treasury.

Chadha's case for the review is simple: the last full, independent look at gambling law was done about a quarter of a century ago, before smartphones, in-play betting on every game, or the online casino boom.

What it is looking at

  • How the 2005 Act works today, and the effect of the reforms that followed the 2023 White Paper (stake limits on online slots, financial risk checks, the statutory levy).
  • Gambling taxation.
  • Illegal and offshore gambling.
  • Gambling-related harm and consumer protection.
  • Gambling and sport.
  • New technology and products.
  • How regulation is coordinated across government.

The commission says it particularly wants empirical evidence and comparisons with other countries.

What it can and cannot do

This is not a government review and it has no power to change the law. That matters: the formal route for change still runs through the Department for Culture, Media and Sport, Parliament and the Gambling Commission, the regulator. What an independent review can do is gather evidence in one place and set out options that ministers and MPs then have to respond to. Reviews of this kind often shape the terms of the next debate.

What it means for punters

Nothing changes for bettors today. But the topics on the list are the ones that touch everyday betting: how affordability and risk checks work, how tax on operators flows through to prices and promotions, and how hard it is for people to end up on unlicensed sites. If you have a view, or data, the commission is asking for it.

How to respond

Submissions go through the commission's website as PDF documents by 12 February 2027. The commission plans to publish what it receives; contributors can mark parts as confidential. No date has been given yet for its final report.
