Free bets explained: stake not returned, expiry and what a free bet is really worth

A £10 free bet is not worth £10. Here is how free bets work in the UK, what the terms mean, the rules bookmakers have to follow, and the simple maths that shows what one is really worth.

Bonuses, rewards and VIPSports betting
By the BetMeisters desk17 November 20267 min read

"Bet £10, get £30 in free bets." It is the most common offer in British betting, and one of the most misunderstood. A free bet is a real thing with real value, but it is almost never worth its face value, and the terms attached to it decide how much you actually get. This guide explains how free bets work, the UK rules that protect you, and how to work out what a free bet is really worth.

One thing first: a free bet is still gambling. Only use offers on bets you were going to make anyway, and only with money you can afford to lose. 18+.

What a free bet is

A free bet is a token that lets you place a bet without staking your own money. If the bet wins, you receive the winnings. If it loses, you lose nothing of your own. That sounds like a straight gift, but there is one big catch, and it is in almost every UK free bet: the stake is not returned.

Stake not returned (SNR): the term that matters most

With a normal £10 bet at 3.00, a win returns £30: £20 profit plus your £10 stake back. With a £10 free bet at 3.00, a win usually returns £20, because the £10 free bet stake is kept by the bookmaker. You get the profit only.

This is why the price you choose matters so much more with a free bet than with cash. At short odds, SNR wipes out most of the value:

A few offers do return the stake. They are rare, and the offer terms will say so clearly. If the terms do not mention the stake, assume it is not returned.

What a free bet is really worth

The fair way to value any bet is its expected value: what it pays on average if you placed it many times. For a free bet with the stake not returned, the formula is simple:

Value of a free bet = free bet amount × chance of winning × (decimal odds − 1)

If the odds were perfectly fair (no bookmaker margin), the chance of winning would be exactly 1 ÷ odds, and the formula becomes free bet amount × (1 − 1 ÷ odds). At fair odds of 2.00 a £10 free bet is worth £5; at fair odds of 10.00 it is worth £9. The longer the odds, the closer the free bet gets to its face value, but it never reaches it.

Real odds include a margin. In BetMeisters' price snapshot on Tuesday morning, the average margin on main win markets at four big UK bookmakers was between about 7.7% and 8.8%, across more than 400 selections. Taking a margin of 8%, the value of a £10 free bet at different prices works out like this:

So a £10 free bet is worth roughly £3 to £8, depending on how you use it. Two practical lessons follow. First, using a free bet on a short-priced favourite is the worst way to use it: you are very likely to win, but you win very little. Second, longer odds get more of the face value, but they also mean you will usually lose. Over a single bet, the outcome is down to luck either way. The maths only tells you the average.

The qualifying bet: what the offer costs you

Most sign-up offers ask you to place a qualifying bet with your own money first: "bet £10, get £30". That qualifying bet carries the normal bookmaker margin. At odds of 2.00 with an 8% margin, a £10 qualifying bet loses about 80p on average. Weigh that against the value of the free bets you receive, and remember that the qualifying bet usually has its own minimum odds.

The bigger risk is not the 80p. It is that an offer gets you to open an account and bet when you would not otherwise have done. If a sign-up offer changes what you would have done, it is costing you more than the maths shows.

The terms to check before you claim

The UK advertising rules say the significant conditions of an offer must be clear in the advert itself or one click away, not buried in small print. In practice, look for these:

  • Eligibility: new customers only, one per person or household, UK and Ireland only, 18+.
  • Qualifying bet: the minimum stake, the minimum odds (often 1/2 or evens), and which markets count. Some offers exclude each-way bets, bet builders or cashed-out bets.
  • Payment method: some offers exclude deposits made with certain e-wallets. This has caught out many people, and the advertising regulator has ruled against offers that hid it.
  • Expiry: free bets usually expire within a set number of days. An unused free bet is worth nothing.
  • Minimum odds and market limits on the free bet itself: many free bets must be used at odds of evens or bigger, or only on certain sports.
  • How it is split: "£30 in free bets" is often three £10 tokens, sometimes for different sports. You may not be able to combine them.
  • Stake returned or not: assume not, unless the terms say otherwise.
  • Wagering on winnings: sports free bet winnings are normally paid as cash you can withdraw. If an offer adds wagering requirements to the winnings, see the 10x cap below.

The UK rules that protect you

Several rules from the UK regulators apply directly to free bets.

"Free" must mean free. The Committee of Advertising Practice's guidance says an offer should not be called "free" if the customer must risk their own money to qualify, and "risk-free" should not be used unless the customer genuinely cannot lose their own money. That is why adverts say "bet £10, get £30 in free bets" rather than "get £30 free".

Wagering is capped at 10 times. Since 19 January 2026, the Gambling Commission caps wagering requirements on gambling promotions at ten. A bonus can no longer require you to re-stake it more than 10 times before you can withdraw. The Commission's own example: a £10 bonus with a 50x requirement used to mean £500 of play.

No mixed-product offers. From the same date, operators cannot offer a promotion that requires you to gamble on two or more types of product, such as "bet on the football and get free spins on slots". The Commission called these potentially harmful.

Restrictions cannot take away an offer you have started. The Gambling Commission's guidance on free bets and account restrictions says that if you have already started placing qualifying bets for a free bet offer, the bookmaker must not then enforce an account restriction (for example, cutting your maximum stake) in a way that stops you getting the free bets or their full value. A restriction notice must also say that it does not affect promotions you have already qualified for.

Free bets for existing customers

Free bets are not only for new accounts. Existing customers often see "bet £20 on the weekend football, get a £5 free bet" offers, acca insurance paid as a free bet, or free bets for a losing streak. The same maths applies: value each one with the formula above, check the minimum odds and expiry, and ask whether the offer is changing what you bet on. A reward that pushes you to bet more often, or on markets you would not normally touch, is working for the bookmaker, not for you.

Common mistakes with free bets

  • Treating the free bet as cash. A £10 free bet is worth roughly £3 to £8, not £10.
  • Using it on a short favourite. At 1.50 a £10 free bet is worth about £3. The same free bet at 4.00 is worth more than twice as much.
  • Missing the expiry date. An expired free bet is worth nothing.
  • Ignoring the qualifying odds. A qualifying bet below the minimum odds does not count, and you will not get the free bets.
  • Chasing offers across many sites. Opening account after account for sign-up offers can quickly turn into betting far more than you meant to.

FAQs

Is a free bet really free? Using it costs you nothing, but getting it usually needs a qualifying bet with your own money, and its value is below its face value because the stake is not returned.

Can I withdraw a free bet? No. You can only bet with it. Winnings from a sports free bet are normally paid as withdrawable cash, minus the stake.

What odds should I use a free bet at? There is no right answer for one bet, but on average a free bet keeps more of its value at bigger odds. On short odds most of the value is lost to stake-not-returned.

Does the 10x wagering cap apply to sports free bets? The Gambling Commission's announcement applies the cap to promotions that come with wagering requirements. Most sports free bets have no wagering requirement on winnings at all; if one does, it cannot be more than 10x.
