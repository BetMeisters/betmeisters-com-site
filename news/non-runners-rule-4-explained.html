Non-runners and Rule 4 explained: what happens to your bet when a horse is withdrawn

Why horses are withdrawn, what happens to bets on the non-runner and on the rest of the field, how a withdrawal moves the market, and the Rule 4 deduction table in fractional, decimal and US odds, with worked examples.

Horse racingSports betting
By the BetMeisters desk26 November 20266 min read

A non-runner is a horse that was declared to run but is withdrawn before the race. It happens most days: on a typical day of British and Irish racing several horses come out, and in big fields there are often two or more in a single race. If you have a bet on the race, a withdrawal can change your bet in three ways: the bet on the withdrawn horse, the price of the horse you backed, and the each-way terms.

Why horses are withdrawn

  • Vet's certificate: the horse is lame, sick or otherwise unfit to run, confirmed by a vet. Welfare comes first.
  • Self-certificate: in Britain a trainer can withdraw a horse without a vet's certificate, for example because the ground has changed. The horse then cannot run again for a short period, and the British Horseracing Authority (BHA) publishes non-runner rates by stable: trainers whose rate is well above the average lose the right to self-certify.
  • The going: rain or a drying track is the most common reason a horse that needs a particular ground is taken out.
  • At the start: a horse that refuses to load, gets loose or is found lame by the starter or the course vet is withdrawn at the start, sometimes minutes before the off.

What happens to your bet on the non-runner

A single on a non-runner is void and your stake is returned. In a multiple (a double, treble or accumulator), the non-runner's leg is treated as void and the bet runs on the other selections: a treble becomes a double, a double becomes a single. The exception is ante-post betting, bets struck days or weeks before the race, usually before final declarations: these are normally 'all in, run or not', so if your horse does not run the stake is lost. Some bookmakers offer non-runner-no-bet on big races; check the terms when you bet.

How a withdrawal moves the market

When a horse comes out, its chance of winning is shared among the rest of the field, so every remaining horse becomes more likely to win and its price shortens. The bigger the chance of the withdrawn horse, the bigger the effect: losing a 7/4 favourite reshapes the whole race, losing a 50/1 outsider barely moves it. On the betting exchanges the market re-forms within seconds; bookmakers then adjust their prices. That is also why a price you took before the withdrawal is no longer fair to the bookmaker, and why Rule 4 exists.

Rule 4 in plain English

Rule 4 (Tattersalls Rule 4(c)) is the deduction bookmakers make from your winnings when a horse is withdrawn after you took a fixed price. It applies to bets struck at a price before the withdrawal was officially announced. The size of the deduction depends on the withdrawn horse's price at the time it came out. Your stake is never reduced: the deduction is taken from the winnings only.

When two or more horses are withdrawn, the deductions are added together, but the total can never be more than 90p in the pound.

Worked examples

  • One withdrawal. You back a horse with £10 at 5/1 (6.00). The 2/1 second favourite is withdrawn after your bet, which means a 25p deduction. Your horse wins: normal winnings would be £50; the deduction is 25% of that, £12.50, so you collect £37.50 winnings plus your £10 stake, £47.50 in total.
  • Two withdrawals. Same bet, but a 7/4 shot (35p) and a 9/2 shot (15p) both come out. The deductions add up to 50p, so your winnings are halved: £25 plus your £10 stake, £35 back.
  • An outsider comes out. A 20/1 horse is withdrawn. Over 14/1 there is no deduction, so you are paid in full.

When Rule 4 does not apply

  • Starting price (SP) bets: the starting price is formed after the market has re-formed without the non-runner, so there is no deduction. The exception is a withdrawal so late that there is no time to form a new market, when the same scale is applied.
  • Ante-post bets struck before final declarations are not subject to Rule 4; but, as above, the stake is usually lost if your horse does not run.
  • Best odds guaranteed: if your bookmaker pays the bigger of your price and the SP, a Rule 4 deduction applies to the price you took; check your bookmaker's terms for how they combine. Our best odds guaranteed guide explains the offer.
  • Walkover: if withdrawals leave only one runner, all bets on the race are void.

Non-runners and each-way bets

Each-way bets are settled on the number of horses that actually start, not the number declared. So a withdrawal can change the place terms:

  • 5 to 7 runners: 1/4 the odds, places 1 and 2.
  • 8 or more runners: 1/5 the odds, places 1, 2 and 3.
  • Handicaps with 12 to 15 runners: 1/4 the odds, three places; 16 to 21 runners: 1/5 the odds, four places; 22 or more: 1/4 the odds, four places.
  • Fewer than 5 runners: usually win only (some bookmakers offer 1/5 the odds for two places with 3 or 4 runners if they say so).

The most painful example is an 8-runner race that loses one horse: three places become two. If your each-way horse finishes third, the place part loses. Some bookmakers keep the original place terms or offer extra places as a promotion; read their rules. Our each way guide has more worked examples. Reserves in big handicaps

Big-field handicaps often name reserves, horses that get a run only if others come out by the deadline. If a reserve takes a withdrawn horse's place, bets struck before the change and the forming of a new market are settled at the starting price under Tattersalls Rule 4(e), not at the price you took. A reserve that does not get a run is not a non-runner.

FAQ

Where can I see today's non-runners? On our non-runners page, updated through the day with the likely Rule 4 deduction for each withdrawal.

Does Rule 4 take money from my stake? No. Only the winnings are reduced; the stake is always returned on a winner.

What is the biggest Rule 4 deduction? 90p in the pound, whether from one heavy odds-on favourite or several withdrawals added together.

My horse was a non-runner in a treble. Do I lose? No. That leg is void and the bet runs as a double on the other two.

Can a non-runner help my bet? Yes, in one way: the remaining horses' chances go up. If you took a price before the withdrawal, Rule 4 shares that gain with the bookmaker; if you bet at SP, the new SP already reflects it.
