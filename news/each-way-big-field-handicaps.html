Each-way in big-field handicaps: place terms, extra places and how to tell when each-way is value

Big handicaps are where each-way betting comes into its own. How the place terms change with the number of runners, what an extra place is worth in numbers, why the same bet can be poor value at four places and good value at five, and the checks to make before the off.

Horse racingSports betting
By the BetMeisters desk28 November 20266 min read

An each-way bet is two equal bets: one on the horse to win and one on it to place. The place part pays a fraction of the win odds if the horse finishes in the paid places. Our each-way explained guide covers the basics. This guide is about where each-way matters most: big-field handicaps, the races with the most places and the most room for value.

These are the standard terms most UK bookmakers use. The number of runners that counts is the number that start: late non-runners can change the terms, and a 16-runner handicap that loses one runner drops from four places at 1/5 to three places at 1/4. Our non-runners guide covers what happens to your bet.

Extra places

On big races, many bookmakers offer extra places: paying five, six or even more places instead of four, usually at the same fraction. Our BOG and extra places guide covers the terms. Here is what one extra place is actually worth.

A worked example: 16 runners, four places v five

Take a 16-runner handicap. We give every horse a win chance (adding to 100%) and work out how often each one finishes in the first four or five, using the standard Harville model: the chance a horse finishes second is its share of the win chances of the horses left once the winner is removed, and so on down the field. We simulated the race 200,000 times.

One horse has a 5% chance of winning (a fair price of 20.00, about 19/1). It is offered at 16/1 (17.00) each-way, 1/5 the odds, so the place part pays at 16/5: 4.20. The model says it finishes in the first four 22.3% of the time, and in the first five 28.9%.

Each-way, 5 placeshalf win, half place+3.2%

The same horse at the same price is a losing bet at four places and a winning one at five. The win part is poor value (-15%) because 16/1 is shorter than its fair price, but the fifth place adds so much to the place part that the whole each-way bet moves into profit. That is why bookmakers ration extra places, and why they are worth looking for.

A word of caution: the Harville model is a simplification. It tends to overstate how often favourites fill the places and understate outsiders, so real place chances for a mid-priced horse can be a little higher than shown. The direction of the result, that extra places add real value, holds.

When each-way is value: the quick checks

  • Mid-priced horses in big fields are where each-way works best: big enough prices for the place fraction to pay well, real chances of a place.
  • Short-priced favourites are usually poor each-way bets: at 3/1 with 1/5 the odds, the place part pays only 3/5 (1.60).
  • Count the runners. 8 runners in a non-handicap (3 places at 1/5) is often good for each-way; so is 16 in a handicap (4 places at 1/5).
  • Watch for non-runners after you bet: terms are fixed by the number that start.
  • Compare the fraction: 1/4 the odds is worth much more than 1/5 on the place part.

Two things stand out. First, place chances grow much faster than win chances for the bigger prices: the 3.5% outsider wins about one race in 29 but makes the first five about one race in 5. Second, each extra place adds less for the favourite than for the mid-priced horses, because the favourite is already in the places most of the time.

The break-even test, step by step

For any each-way bet you can check each half on its own:

  1. Win part: break-even win chance = 1 ÷ decimal price. At 16/1 (17.00) that is 5.9%.
  2. Place part: place price = 1 + (decimal price − 1) × fraction. At 16/1 and 1/5 that is 1 + 16/5 = 4.20, so break-even place chance = 1 ÷ 4.20 = 23.8%.
  3. Compare each with your own estimate (or the exchange's win and place markets, with commission taken off).
  4. Add the two halves together. One half can be poor value and the bet still be good overall, as in the example above. If both halves are poor, it is a poor bet.

The sweet spots bookmakers know about

Each-way terms are set by runner numbers, not by how open the race is, so some field sizes are kinder to the backer than others. The classic ones are an eight-runner non-handicap with a short-priced favourite (three places at 1/5 in a race where only a few have a real chance), and a 16-runner handicap (four places at 1/5) where the field is bunched. Bookmakers know this too, which is why prices in those races are often trimmed, and why some firms pay fewer places on certain races. Always check the terms on the bet slip, not just the race.

Dead heats and Rule 4

If your horse dead-heats for the last place paid, the place part is usually settled at half the stake at the full place price (dead-heat rules). And if a horse is withdrawn after you bet, a Rule 4 deduction comes off your winnings on both parts, while the place terms are fixed by the runners that start. A non-runner can therefore cut both your price and your number of places.

Common questions

How much does a £5 each-way bet cost? £10: £5 on the win and £5 on the place.

What happens if my horse wins? Both parts pay: the win at the full odds, the place at the fraction.

What is a 'place only' bet? A bet on the place alone, sometimes offered at different terms. Check it against the each-way place fraction.

Are extra places worth less if the fraction drops? Yes: five places at 1/5 can be worth less than four at 1/4. Compare both.
