Draw no bet and double chance explained: what they cover, how they are priced and when they make sense

Two ways to take the draw out of a football bet: one gives your stake back if it is a draw, the other wins if it is a draw. How each settles, how to work out a fair price from the match odds, and the margin trap to watch for.

FootballSports betting
By the BetMeisters desk28 November 20265 min read

In a football match result market there are three outcomes: home win, draw, away win. The draw is the outcome most bettors forget, and it happens in roughly a quarter of league matches. Draw no bet and double chance are two different ways of dealing with it. They sound similar but pay out very differently, so it is worth knowing exactly what each one does.

Draw no bet in one line

You pick a team. If they win, you win. If it is a draw, you get your stake back. If they lose, you lose.

Draw no bet (often written DNB) removes the draw as an outcome. In Asian handicap terms it is exactly the same bet as a 0 handicap (sometimes written 0.0 or 'level ball'). If you have read our Asian handicaps guide, DNB is the simplest line on that ladder.

  • Your team wins: paid at the DNB price.
  • Draw: stake returned (a void, or 'push').
  • Your team loses: stake lost.

Double chance in one line

You pick two of the three outcomes. If either happens, you win.

There are three double chance bets in every match:

  • 1X: home win or draw.
  • X2: away win or draw.
  • 12: home win or away win (anything but a draw).

Because you cover two outcomes, double chance prices are short: usually odds-on. You win more often, but you win less each time.

The difference that matters

Back the away team 'X2' and the match is drawn: you win. Back the same team 'draw no bet' and it is drawn: you get your stake back. So double chance treats the draw as a winner and charges you for it in a shorter price. Draw no bet treats the draw as nothing happening, and the price is longer.

A simple way to remember it: double chance includes the draw; draw no bet ignores the draw.

A real example: Luton v Oxford

Before Saturday's League One match between Luton and Oxford, the sharp market (Pinnacle's prices with the margin removed) made it: Luton 55.2%, draw 23.2%, Oxford 21.6%. Here is what those chances mean for fair prices in each market:

Notice how much the draw moves the underdog's price. Oxford to win was a fair 4.63. Take the draw out (DNB) and it is 3.56. Add the draw to your side (X2) and it is 2.23.

How to work out a fair price yourself

You only need the three chances from the match result market, ideally with the bookmaker's margin removed (see our odds converter for turning prices into chances).

  • Draw no bet: fair price = (1 − draw chance) ÷ team's chance. For Luton: (1 − 0.232) ÷ 0.552 = 1.39.
  • Double chance: fair price = 1 ÷ (the two chances added together). For Oxford or draw: 1 ÷ (0.216 + 0.232) = 2.23.

A quick shortcut that works from any bookmaker's match odds: DNB price ≈ team price × (1 − 1 ÷ draw price). With Luton at 1.81 and the draw at 4.31, that gives 1.81 × (1 − 0.232) = 1.39. Because it uses the bookmaker's own prices it inherits their margin, so treat it as a guide, not the fair price.

The margin trap

Every market carries the bookmaker's margin, but it is not always spread evenly. Double chance prices are short, and a few hundredths off a short price is a bigger slice of your return than it looks. On a fair price of 1.28, being offered 1.22 means giving up about 4% of the value on every bet. Before taking a double chance or DNB price, work out the fair price from the match odds and compare. If the bookmaker's DNB is noticeably shorter than the match result maths says it should be, you are paying extra for the insurance.

When each one makes sense

  • Draw no bet suits a team you think is underrated but where a draw is a real possibility: you keep your stake if it ends level. It is a natural choice in tight, low-scoring leagues where draws are common.
  • Double chance suits a view that one team will not lose, rather than that it will win. Its prices are short, so it only makes sense when the price is still longer than the combined chance.
  • 12 (no draw) is a bet against a draw. It tends to suit matches between two attacking sides, and it is worth checking against the 'both teams to score' and goals markets, which carry related information.

Neither market is 'safer' in a way that matters. A lower-risk bet at a bad price still loses money over time. What matters is the same as for any bet: is the price longer than the real chance?

Common questions

Is draw no bet the same as Asian handicap 0? Yes. Both return your stake on a draw.

Does extra time count? Usually not. Most football markets, including DNB and double chance, settle on 90 minutes plus stoppage time. Check the market's rules for cup ties.

What happens in a double with a DNB leg that draws? The leg is void and the double becomes a single on the other leg, at most bookmakers. Rules vary, so check.
